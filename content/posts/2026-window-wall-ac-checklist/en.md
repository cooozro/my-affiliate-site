## Do these in order, not all at once

Shopping for a window or wall air conditioner in the middle of a heat wave is the worst time to make a decision. The pressure to buy something — anything — that blows cold air leads to the two most common regrets we hear from readers: the unit is too weak for the room, or it doesn't fit the window frame at all. This checklist walks you through the decisions in the order they actually matter, so you don't waste money on the wrong size or an install that falls through.

Start with the room, not the machine. Measure the floor area in square feet (or meters), note the ceiling height, and think about how much direct sun the room gets. Then check your window or wall opening dimensions. Only after you know those two numbers should you start comparing BTU ratings and efficiency labels. If you reverse that order, you'll end up with a great deal on a unit that's either too big (short-cycling, high humidity) or too small (running nonstop, never reaching the set temperature).

We cross-checked public spec sheets from LG, Frigidaire, Midea, and GE, plus listed retail prices and owner reviews, to build the thresholds below. This is a spec-and-review cross-check, not a hands-on lab test — but the sizing rules are well established by the DOE and AHAM, and they haven't changed much in years.

## Pre-purchase checks

1. **Measure the room and calculate the BTU you actually need**

The single most important number is the cooling load, measured in British Thermal Units per hour (BTU/h). A common rule of thumb is about 20 BTU per square foot of living space, but that assumes average ceiling height and moderate sun. For a 200 sq ft bedroom, that's 4,000 BTU; for a 400 sq ft living room, 8,000 BTU.

**Why it matters:** An undersized unit runs constantly, drives up your electric bill, and struggles to dehumidify. An oversized unit cools the air quickly but shuts off before removing enough moisture, leaving the room feeling clammy.

**Red flag:** If a sales page or review says "one size fits all" or gives a huge range like "covers up to 450 sq ft" for a 5,000 BTU unit, treat it with suspicion. Always check the AHAM-certified coverage area on the spec sheet.

2. **Check your window or wall opening dimensions before you fall in love**

Window ACs come in standard widths — 23–25 inches is typical for 5,000–8,000 BTU units, while larger units (10,000 BTU and up) may need 26–30 inches. Measure the width, height, and depth of your window opening, and check whether it's a single-hung, double-hung, or sliding window. Wall sleeves have their own dimensions, usually 24–26 inches wide and 15–16 inches high.

**Why it matters:** A unit that's too wide won't fit; one that's too narrow leaves gaps that let hot air and insects in. The mounting kit must match your window type — a sliding window needs a different bracket than a double-hung.

**Red flag:** If the product page doesn't list the minimum and maximum window opening dimensions, or if the unit is heavy (over 70 lbs) and you plan to install it alone, that's a warning. Many retailers offer free installation, but verify the service area first.

3. **Understand the efficiency label: EER, CEER, and the Energy Star mark**

In the U.S., window ACs are rated by Combined Energy Efficiency Ratio (CEER), which replaced the older EER for most units. Higher CEER means better efficiency — look for 12 or above for a 5,000–8,000 BTU unit, and 11 or above for larger units. The Energy Star mark means the unit meets the EPA's stricter efficiency criteria, which can save you 10–15% on cooling costs.

**Why it matters:** Efficiency directly affects your monthly electric bill. A CEER of 12 vs. 10 can save roughly $30–50 per season for a typical bedroom unit, depending on your local electricity rates.

**Red flag:** If the spec sheet lists only EER and not CEER, check the model year — the DOE updated the test procedure in 2023, and older ratings are not directly comparable. Also, be wary of units that claim "high efficiency" without a specific number.

4. **Look at the real-world noise level, not just the spec sheet**

Noise is measured in decibels (dB). A unit rated at 50 dB is quiet enough for a bedroom; 60 dB is noticeable but acceptable for a living room; 70 dB is loud enough to interfere with conversation. Owner reviews often report that "low" mode is much quieter than "high," so check the range, not just the max.

**Why it matters:** A noisy AC can disrupt sleep and make the room unusable for work or study. The compressor and fan noise are the two main sources, and some units have better insulation than others.

**Red flag:** If the spec sheet lists a single dB number without a mode, or if reviews consistently mention a rattling or buzzing sound that isn't in the marketing material, factor that in.

5. **Check the electrical requirements: 115V vs. 230V, and dedicated circuits**

Most small window ACs (up to 8,000 BTU) run on a standard 115V/15-amp household circuit. Larger units (12,000 BTU and up) often need a 230V outlet and a dedicated circuit. Wall-mounted units may have similar requirements. Check your breaker panel and outlet type before buying.

**Why it matters:** If you buy a 230V unit but only have a 115V outlet, you'll need an electrician to run a new circuit — a significant extra cost. Conversely, plugging a high-wattage unit into a shared circuit can trip breakers.

**Red flag:** If the product page doesn't clearly state the voltage and amperage, or if the plug type is unusual (e.g., a 20-amp plug), verify compatibility with your home's wiring.

6. **Consider the filter and maintenance routine**

All window and wall ACs have a washable air filter that should be cleaned every two weeks during heavy use. Some models have a filter-check indicator light. The filter is usually a foam or mesh panel that slides out from the front.

**Why it matters:** A dirty filter restricts airflow, reducing cooling capacity and efficiency. It also makes the unit work harder, which can lead to ice buildup on the coils.

**Red flag:** If the filter is difficult to access or requires tools to remove, you're less likely to clean it regularly. Also, check if the unit has a permanent filter (washable) or a disposable one that needs replacement.

7. **Think about installation and support before you commit**

Window AC installation can be a DIY job if you have a standard window and a friend to help lift. But if you live in an apartment with restrictions on window units, or if your window is an unusual size or on a high floor, professional installation is safer. Wall units require cutting a hole in the wall, which is a bigger project.

**Why it matters:** Improper installation can void the warranty, cause leaks, and even create a fall hazard. Also, check the warranty length and whether it covers the compressor (usually 5 years) and parts/labor (often 1 year).

**Red flag:** If the retailer charges extra for removal of your old unit, or if the warranty doesn't cover the refrigerant charge, factor that into the total cost.

## Consumables and three-year cost

Beyond the purchase price, plan for these recurring costs over three years:

- **Electricity:** A typical 8,000 BTU window AC uses about 600–800 kWh per season, which at $0.15/kWh is $90–120 per year. A more efficient unit (CEER 12+) saves about $30–50 per year.
- **Filters:** Most have washable filters, so the only consumable is cleaning supplies (about $10 per year). Some models use disposable filters that cost $10–20 each, replaced twice a year.
- **Maintenance:** Annual cleaning of the coils and fins (DIY or professional) costs $50–150 if you hire a pro.
- **Repairs:** A compressor failure within the first 5 years is covered by warranty, but after that, repairs can cost $200–400. Consider an extended warranty if you plan to keep the unit long-term.

**Total cost of ownership example:** For a $400 window AC with a CEER of 12, over three years you might spend $400 (unit) + $300 (electricity savings vs. a less efficient unit) + $30 (cleaning supplies) + $150 (professional cleaning once) = about $880. A less efficient unit (CEER 10) could cost $400 (unit) + $450 (electricity) + $30 + $150 = $1,030. The efficient unit saves roughly $150 over three years, not counting the environmental benefit.

## FAQ

### How do I calculate the BTU needed for my room?

Multiply the square footage of the room by 20, then adjust for ceiling height (add 10% if the ceiling is over 8 feet) and sun exposure (add 10% if the room faces south or west). For example, a 300 sq ft bedroom with average sun would need about 6,000 BTU. If you're not sure, use the AHAM's online sizing calculator or consult the manufacturer's coverage chart.

### Can I install a window AC in a sliding window?

Yes, but you need a mounting kit designed for sliding windows. These kits use a bracket that fits into the window track and secures the unit vertically. Some units come with such a kit included, but others require a separate purchase. Measure the window's width and height carefully, and check the product description for "sliding window compatible."

### What's the difference between EER and CEER?

EER (Energy Efficiency Ratio) measures cooling output divided by electrical input under a steady-state condition. CEER (Combined Energy Efficiency Ratio) includes standby power and is measured under a more realistic test procedure that the DOE adopted in 2023. CEER is generally lower than EER for the same unit, so don't compare them directly. Look for CEER on the EnergyGuide label for new units.

### How often should I clean the filter?

During heavy use (daily running), clean the filter every two weeks. A simple vacuum or rinse with water is enough. If you have pets or live in a dusty area, clean it weekly. A dirty filter can reduce efficiency by up to 15% and cause the unit to freeze up.

### Is it worth paying more for an Energy Star certified window AC?

Yes, if you plan to use the unit for more than a few weeks a year. Energy Star models are about 10–15% more efficient than standard models, which can save $30–50 per season on electricity. Over a 5-year lifespan, that's $150–250 in savings, often offsetting the higher upfront cost. Plus, some utilities offer rebates for Energy Star purchases.

## What to decide today

The most urgent decision is not which brand to buy — it's whether a window AC or a wall-mounted unit fits your space and your electrical setup. Measure your window or wall opening, calculate the BTU you need, and check the voltage requirements. If you're in a rental, confirm that window units are allowed. Once you have those three numbers, you can narrow down to a shortlist of models that actually fit.

For most single rooms up to 400 sq ft, a 5,000–8,000 BTU window AC with a CEER of 12 or higher is the sweet spot. If you have a permanent wall opening or want a cleaner look, a wall-mounted unit is worth the higher install cost. Avoid buying the cheapest unit without checking the efficiency label — the savings on your electric bill will pay for a better model within a season.
